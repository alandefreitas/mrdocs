### `[Freitas2026]`

Alan de Freitas, "Giving talks", North task 778, note 110, 2026-09-10. Own notes accumulated over years of talks, including the pattern used at GECCO 2019 where the presentation won the award. Evidence: (own). Read in full. No references. Seeds every section of the synthesis; the full catalogue of opening lines it contains is reproduced here so the synthesis can stay short:

- Story openers: "I grew up to study the brain because I have a brother who..." / "We grew up interacting with the physical objects around us..." / "If we're gonna dive in the deep sea, and..." / "I've been triggered by this question of whether we could evolve or develop a sixth sense." / "About ten years ago, I took on the task to teach global development to Swedish undergraduate students." / "So, I'll start with this: a few years ago, an event planner called me because I was going to do a speaking event. And he said..."
- Direct statement: "There's nothing bigger or older than the universe. The questions I would like to talk about are..." / "So as researchers, something we often do is use immense resources to achieve certain goals."
- Provocative: "I want to discuss with you this afternoon why you're going to fail to have a great career."
- Curiosity: "I need to make a confession at the outset here. A little over 20 years ago I did something that I regret."
- Shocking: "You will live seven and a half minutes longer than you would have otherwise, just because you watched this talk."
- Quote: "I felt a funeral in my brain and..." (Emily Dickinson). We know depression from metaphors.
- Captivating visual: "At TED we talk a lot about leadership and how to make a movement. So let's make a movement happen, start to finish, in under three minutes." / "I'm going to show you a couple of images from a study..."
- Question: "Do you think it's possible to control someone's attention? Even more than that, what about predicting human behavior?" / "How do you explain when things don't go as we assume?" / "Brain magic. What's brain magic all about?"
- Physical object: "When I was 9, I went to summer camp for the first time and my mum packed me a suitcase." (Have the suitcase.)
- Joke: "Okay, now I don't want to alarm anybody in this room, but it's just come to my attention that the person to your right is a liar." / "When you have 22 minutes to speak, 2 million years seem like a really long time."
- Imagine: "Imagine a big explosion as you climb through 3,000 feet. Imagine a plane full of smoke. Imagine an engine going clack, clack, clack."
- Personal experience: "I have to tell you I'm both challenged and excited." / "I'm honored to be with you for your commencement from one of the finest universities in the world. Truth be told, I never graduated from college." / "How are you? It's been great, hasn't it? There have been three themes in this conference." / "I'm a writer. Writing books is my profession, but it's more than that." / "I'm really excited to be here today. I'm about to show you some stuff that just came out of the lab."
- Closings: "The lesson I want to leave you with from this data is that our worries are always to some degree overblown and ___." / "We must do this and that." / "The ultimate goal is to ___ and we are making good progress." / "If anyone wants to discuss how we can improve ___, I'll be outside." / "That's all I have." / "I rest my case." / "I hope you enjoyed ___." / "Thank you for listening."
- Open item in the note: add the reference to memes and stories (Pinocchio and the others).

### `[Mayer2003]`

Mayer, R. E., and Moreno, R. (2003). Nine ways to reduce cognitive load in multimedia learning. *Educational Psychologist* 38(1), 43-52. Read in full (11 pages, PDF). Evidence: a review of the authors' own 12-year programme of controlled experiments at UCSB, reporting median effect sizes and the number of experiments behind each (Table 3); so (meta) where several experiments back an effect, (exp) where one does. Outcome measure throughout is problem-solving *transfer* after a short narrated animation, with undergraduates, so generalisation to conference talks is by mechanism, not by direct test. Key numbers taken into the synthesis: modality 1.17 (6 exps), segmenting 1.36 (1), pretraining 1.00 (3), coherence 0.90 (5), signalling 0.74 (1), spatial contiguity 0.48 (1), redundancy 0.69 (3), temporal contiguity 1.30 (8), spatial-ability interaction 1.13 (2). Also the nuance that on-screen text *helps* when no animation competes (Moreno and Mayer 2002, exps 1 and 3).

Cites, relevant: Sweller (1999) cognitive load theory; Chandler and Sweller (1991); Paivio (1986) dual coding; Baddeley (1998) working memory; Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003) expertise reversal effect; Mautone and Mayer (2001) signalling; Moreno and Mayer (2002) verbal redundancy; Mayer (2001) *Multimedia Learning*; Hegarty and Just (1989) eye movements between text and diagram. Cited by about 4,100 works (Semantic Scholar); the citation list was not retrievable through the API at the DOI record and is pending. Queued from here: `[Kalyuga2003]` (does the redundancy effect reverse for expert audiences? relevant because conference audiences are experts), `[Moreno2002]` (the text-helps-when-no-picture result at first hand).

### `[Kosslyn2012]`

Kosslyn, S. M., Kievit, R. A., Russell, A. G., and Shephard, J. M. (2012). PowerPoint presentation flaws and failures: a psychological analysis. *Frontiers in Psychology* 3:230. Read pages 1-20 of 38 (introduction, principles, all three studies, discussion, references); pages 21-38 are the appendix table of the Study 2 survey items and were not read. Evidence: Study 1 (obs, 140 slide decks sampled from the web, scored against 137 rules by four judges, inter-rater agreement 0.88); Study 2 (obs, online survey of 205 people who regularly attend slide presentations); Study 3 (exp, 48 participants choosing between slide pairs and explaining the choice). The eight principles and their corollaries are the authors' synthesis of the perception and memory literature, so they are marked (expert) in the synthesis unless a study measured them. Authors' own caveat: the rules have face validity; their effect on comprehension was not tested directly, and a talented presenter may compensate for some flaws.

Cites, relevant: Levasseur and Sawyer (2006) review; Savoy, Proctor and Salvendy (2009) retention from PowerPoint vs traditional lectures (students recalled more of what was said verbally in the traditional lecture); Susskind (2005, 2008) students like PowerPoint and feel more efficacious but do not behave differently; Apperson, Laws and Scepansky (2006, 2008) student preferences, including lists built one item at a time; Mahar, Yaylacicegi and Janicki (2009) "The dark side of custom animation": one-at-a-time lists improved memory for only two of nine items; Bartsch and Cobern (2003); Gunel, Hand and Gunduz (2006) PowerPoint beat chapter summaries for physics; Cowan (2001) working memory holds about four chunks; Tversky, Morrison and Betrancourt (2002) "Animation: can it facilitate?"; Wagoner (2008) and Karns et al. ### `[Sugimoto2013]`

Sugimoto, C. R., Thelwall, M., Larivière, V., Tsou, A., Mongeon, P., and Macaluso, B. (2013). Scientists popularizing science: characteristics and impact of TED talk presenters. *PLOS ONE* 8(4): e62403. Read in full (8 pages). Evidence: (obs), bibliometric and webometric analysis of all 1,202 TED talks online in April 2012 (998 unique presenters, 206 academics), with YouTube and ted.com popularity metrics. It is about *who* gives popular talks, not *how*, so it contributes little to craft; what it does contribute is a null result on prestige. Findings taken into the synthesis: neither the rank of the presenter's university nor their academic age correlated with any popularity metric; academics' talks drew more comments and a higher like ratio but not more views; men's talks were viewed more on YouTube (audience composition, not craft); giving a TED talk did not increase the presenter's citations afterwards. Also the observation that TED condenses a body of work into 18 minutes or less ("episodic framing", citing Iyengar 1991).

Cites, relevant: Thelwall, Sud and Vis (2012) commenting on YouTube videos; TED Blog (2012) "What talks have resonated most with you?"; Haran and Poliakoff (2012) "The periodic table of videos", Science; Iyengar (1991) episodic framing. Cited-by list pending (Semantic Scholar query in progress). Queued: `[Tsou2014]` (Tsou, Thelwall, Mongeon, Sugimoto, "A community of curious souls: an analysis of commenting behavior on TED talks", PLOS ONE 2014) for what audiences actually respond to in comments.

### `[Winston2018]`

Winston, P. H. (2018). How to Speak. MIT OpenCourseWare RES.TLL-005, January IAP 2018. Lecture transcript, 22 pages, read in full. His own ending demonstrates the alternatives to "thank you": the benediction, the *ite missa est*, and the salute to the audience, and he closes with a salute ("I'm glad you're here... I salute you for that"). Evidence: (expert); Winston taught this lecture at MIT for over forty years. Where he reports data it is anecdotal (a student's informal experiment on slide text versus speech; student surveys asking for "more chalk, less PowerPoint") and is marked as such. Notable disagreements with `[Freitas2026]` recorded in the synthesis: don't open with a joke (the audience is still adjusting to your voice); don't end with "thank you" (it suggests they stayed out of politeness). No references in the usual sense; he names Seymour Papert, Alan Lazarus, Doug Lenat and Ibsen's *Hedda Gabler* as sources of technique.

### `[Aippersbach2013]`

Aippersbach, S. M., Alley, M., and Garner, J. K. (2013). How slide design affects a student presenter's understanding of the content. *ASEE Annual Conference*, Atlanta, paper 5691. Read in full (10 pages plus appendices; the file was mislabelled in the queue as Garner 2011). Evidence: (exp), two groups of more than 50 engineering students each built five slides on MRI from the same article, one group instructed in assertion-evidence minutes before, one free to choose (and choosing topic-subtopic); unannounced essay quiz one day later, two blind raters, 22-item rubric; about 10% of submissions excluded for mixing the two structures. Results: total 12.55 vs 10.68 out of 20, t(51) = 2.62, p = 0.01; on the most complex step 2.96 vs 1.97 out of 5, F(1,52) = 9.92, p < .01; misconceptions 0.93 vs 1.12; the topic-subtopic group recalled marginally more secondary details. Small study, one site, same group as the design's authors. Cites, relevant: Garner et al. (2009) for the 85% phrase-headline figure; Paivio dual coding; Alley (2013) *The Craft of Scientific Presentations*. Cited-by: pending.

### `[Garner2013]`

Garner, J. K., and Alley, M. (2013). How the design of presentation slides affects audience comprehension: a case for the assertion-evidence approach. *International Journal of Engineering Education* 29(6), 1564-1579. Read in full (16 pages). Evidence: (exp), randomised: 110 engineering undergraduates (59 assertion-evidence, 51 common practice), identical 1,000-word narration pre-recorded at 141 words per minute, eight minutes, eleven slides per condition (mean 21.2 words per assertion-evidence slide, 41.5 per common-practice slide, no animation). Measures: immediate essay (blind rubric, 90% inter-rater agreement), immediate multiple choice, self-rated effort and prior knowledge, unannounced fill-in test ten days later. Results taken into the synthesis: essay total d = 0.81 (p < .001); four of five process segments significant (d 0.34 to 0.82); dynamic/causal terms d = 0.60; total misconceptions d = 0.47; immediate multiple choice: no main effect, but an interaction with question type, assertion-evidence better on inference questions (partial eta-squared 0.09) and no worse on facts; delayed test d = 0.89; perceived mental effort lower, d = 0.50, and effort correlated negatively with learning independent of prior knowledge; common-practice participants rated their slides' amount of text as more appropriate (d = 0.60) despite learning less. Assertion-evidence participants self-rated slightly higher prior knowledge, which did not correlate with outcomes. Acknowledges Richard Mayer's comments on the draft. Same research group as the design.

Cites, relevant: Savoy et al. (2009); Susskind (2005); Mayer (2005) principles chapter; Chandler and Sweller (1991); Cooper (2009) "Overloading on slides"; Vik (2004) "Breaking bad habits"; Mayer and Anderson (1991) animations need narrations; Moreno and Mayer (1999) modality and contiguity; Harp and Mayer (1997) seductive details; Mayer, Griffith, Jurkowitz and Rothman (2008) interestingness of extraneous details decreases learning; Tangen et al. ### `[Alley2006]`

Alley, M., Schreiber, M., Ramsdell, K., and Muffo, J. (2006). How the design of headlines in presentation slides affects audience retention. *Technical Communication* 53(2), 225-234. Read in full (10 pages). Evidence: (exp, quasi-experimental): four sections of an introductory geoscience course at Virginia Tech (200, 202, 201 and 136 students), same instructor, same content; two sections taught from mostly phrase-headline slides, two from sentence-headline transformations of the same slides (about 100 slides transformed; bodies also made more visual, which the authors flag as a confound they could not isolate). Outcome: final-exam questions whose answer resided in a sentence headline (15 questions). Results: 79% vs 69% correct, chi-square p < .001; 7 of 15 questions significantly higher, 2 significantly lower; on 40 control questions the sentence-headline sections scored *lower* (82.5% and 79.1% vs 87.9% and 86.1%), so the recall gain came despite less studying; 23 questions whose answers were in slide bodies or images showed no difference (86% vs 87%, 73% vs 74%). Single-slide contrasts: phrase 46% vs sentence 63%; none 23% vs sentence 57%; question 24% vs sentence 58%. Authors note Mayer's studies use scripted narration and that live technical talks differ (unscripted speech, persuasion, credibility-building detail). Cites, relevant: Perry (1978) audience requirements for technical speakers (the Hughes Aircraft origin of sentence headlines); Gottlieb (2002) Lawrence Livermore; Alley and Neeley (2005); Doumont (2005); Atkinson (2005) *Beyond Bullet Points*; Mayer (2001); Shaw, Brown and Bromiley (1998) "Strategic stories: how 3M is rewriting business planning" (bullets remove hierarchy); Toulmin (2003) *The Uses of Argument*. Cited-by: pending. Queued: `[Shaw1998]`.

### `[Blome2017]`

Blome, C., Sondermann, H., and Augustin, M. (2017). Accepted standards on how to give a medical research presentation: a systematic review of expert opinion papers. *GMS Journal for Medical Education* 34(1), Doc11. Read in full (the English original, pages 1-7; pages 8-15 are the German translation). Evidence: (obs) as a systematic review of *advice*, not of outcomes: PubMed title search 1975 to July 2015, 4,140 hits, 91 expert-opinion articles included (63 medical, 28 nursing and allied), 3,135 recommendations extracted, 679 distinct after grouping, 29 given by at least 20% of articles (Table 1, used throughout the synthesis as "expert consensus" with the percentage). The only outright contradiction among the 29 was light versus dark slide background (26.4% dark, 3.3% light); smaller disagreements on clip art, pointers and building a slide up sequentially. Font sizes recommended ranged 18 to 32 points, 24 most often. The discussion is the valuable part for this document: the most frequent advice, keep slides simple and limit text, is *not* supported by the experiments they could find (Wecker 2012; Katt et al. 2008; Blokzijl and Andeweg 2005 found no retention difference between concise and detailed slides), whereas irrelevant pictures hurting recall (Bartsch and Cobern 2003) and eye contact helping credibility, comprehensibility and a later test (Blokzijl and Andeweg 2005, confounded with liveliness and not reading) are supported. They propose four presenter-relevant outcomes for future experiments: learning, attitude change, interest, and the presenter's reputation. The 24.2% recommendation to put phrases rather than sentences on slides is recorded in the synthesis as a disagreement with `[Alley2006]` `[Garner2013]`.

### `[Moulton2017]`

Moulton, S. T., Türkay, S., and Kosslyn, S. M. (2017). Does a presentation's medium affect its message? PowerPoint, Prezi, and oral presentations. *PLOS ONE* 12(7): e0178774. Read in full (39 pages). Funded by a grant from Prezi to Kosslyn; the authors document the funder's absence from design, analysis and publication decisions, and the results include several that are not flattering to any format. Evidence: (exp), and the only study found so far that randomises *presenters* to formats. Experiment 1: 146 presenters (screened for experience with all three formats) randomly assigned to Prezi, PowerPoint or no visuals, 30 minutes to study a Harvard Business School case, 45 minutes to prepare a sub-five-minute pitch, delivered live over Skype to 153 audience participants who rated each on organised, engaging, persuasive, effective (1 to 5) and ranked them. Prezi beat both other formats on every dimension (pairwise p between .017 and .0001, differences 0.42 to 0.74); PowerPoint and oral did not differ on any (p from .10 to .89). Presenters' self-rated comprehension, effort, efficacy and their multiple-choice scores on the case did not differ by format. Free responses: 49% of those preferring Prezi cited engaging/visual, 38% of those preferring PowerPoint cited concise/clear/familiar, and those preferring oral cited connection, eye contact and undivided attention. Experiment 2: 1,069 Mechanical Turk participants each saw one recorded presentation (25 per format, blind-judged for recording quality) or an unrelated control video, then decided the business case. Prezi again rated higher than both on organisation, effectiveness, engagement and persuasion, and Prezi presenters as more organised, knowledgeable, effective and professional; PowerPoint and oral did not differ. Decision to accept the offer: Prezi 53.7%, PowerPoint 49.8%, oral 45.5%, control 37.5% (omnibus p = .002; Prezi vs control p = .0003; PowerPoint vs control p = .006; oral vs control p = .07; Prezi vs oral p = .06; Prezi vs PowerPoint p = .39). No format changed recall or comprehension of the case. Ratings of presentation and presenter correlated with the decision (d = .93, .83, .78). Viewers who judged text "too much" or "not enough", or graphs, images or animation "not enough", rated the talk worse and rejected the offer more; PowerPoint was judged to lack animation most (p = .001). The authors argue the mechanism is the panning-and-zooming animation of a single canvas, not the software, and contrast it with slideware's superfluous transitions as "seductive details". Their literature review (Table 1) codes 66 prior studies: 71% did not control audience self-selection, 100% did not control presenter self-selection, 82% studied the researchers' own students; of 28 PowerPoint-versus-traditional performance studies, 17 null, 9 favour PowerPoint, 2 favour traditional; 21 of 26 perception studies favour PowerPoint.

Cites, relevant (of 107): Levasseur and Sawyer (2006); Tufte (2003); Kjeldsen (2013) visual argumentation in Al Gore's slideshow; Szabo and Hastings (2000); Bartsch and Cobern (2003); Savoy et al. (2009); Susskind (2005, 2008); Apperson et al. (2008); Guadagno, Sundie, Hardison and Cialdini (2011) "The persuasive power of PowerPoint presentations"; Park and Feigenson (2013) "Effects of a visual technology on mock juror decision making" (jurors more persuaded by, and remembering more from, attorneys using slides); Kahneman (2011); Nesbit and Adesope (2006) concept-map meta-analysis; Mayer (2002); Moreno and Mayer (1999); Tversky et al. (2002) "Animation: can it facilitate?"; Turkay and Moulton (2016) whiteboard animations preferred over slideware in online lectures; Zongker and Salesin (2003) on creating animated presentations; Chou, Chang and Lu (2015) Prezi vs PowerPoint vs blackboard; Casteleyn et al. (2013); Petty and Cacioppo (1986) dual-process persuasion; Hegarty (2011) cognitive science of visual-spatial displays. Cited-by: pending. Queued: `[Park2013]`, `[Guadagno2011]`, `[Turkay2016]`, `[Nesbit2006]`, `[Tversky2002]` (already queued).

### `[Naegle2021]`

Naegle, K. M. (2021). Ten simple rules for effective presentation slides. *PLOS Computational Biology* 17(12): e1009554. Read in full (6 pages). Evidence: (expert), an editorial by a practising researcher; cites `[Kosslyn2012]` and `[Mayer2003]` for the cognitive rationale and Mayer and Johnson (2008) for the claim that short redundant text improves retention. The ten rules: one idea per slide; one minute per slide; use the heading to state the message; only essential points; give credit consistently; use graphics, almost never text-only slides; design against cognitive overload (no full sentences, few elements, short guide-post text, accessible type and colour, rehearse with captions); design so a distracted person still gets the takeaway; iterate through practice, ideally in front of peers; design against technical disasters (PDF copy, still-image backups for videos, avoid host-dependent animation). Records the "six elements" claim from David JP Phillips's TEDx talk without a source. Recorded in the synthesis as a disagreement with the assertion-evidence group on full sentences, and reconciled there (one sentence as headline, none in the body).

Cites, relevant (of 13): Lortie (2017) "Ten simple rules for short and swift presentations", PLOS Comput Biol; Bourne (2007); Kosslyn et al. (2012); Phillips (2014) TEDxStockholmSalon "How to avoid death by PowerPoint"; Mayer and Moreno (2003); Mayer and Johnson (2008) "Revising the redundancy principle in multimedia learning", J Educ Psychol 100; Reynolds (2011) *Presentation Zen* 2nd ed.; Tufte (2001) *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*. Cited-by: pending. Queued: `[Mayer2008]` (the revision of redundancy; decides whether one-word slides are a benefit or merely harmless), `[Lortie2017]`.

### `[Tsou2014]`

Tsou, A., Thelwall, M., Mongeon, P., and Sugimoto, C. R. (2014). A community of curious souls: an analysis of commenting behavior on TED Talks videos. *PLOS ONE* 9(4): e93609. Read in full (11 pages). Evidence: (obs), content analysis of comments. Stage 1: 405 science-and-technology TED videos, three comments per platform each, two coders (kappa fair-to-good on major categories). Stage 2: 196 videos stratified by presenter gender and academic status, fifteen oldest comments per platform, 5,854 comments, coded for target (speaker, talk, other commenter, TED, other) and sentiment. Findings: comments on the TED site engage with the talk's content more than on YouTube (72.7% vs 56.7%; 85.3% vs 60.8% in stage 2), YouTube has more commenter-to-commenter interaction and insults; across both, about three quarters of comments engage with content regardless of who the speaker is. Women presenters drew more comments about the speaker (15.3% vs 9.8%), both more positive and more negative; non-academics likewise drew more speaker comments and warmer sentiment; academics drew more neutral discussion of content. Contribution to the synthesis is small and goes into the "who you are" claim in Section 1.

Cites, relevant: Sugimoto et al. (2013); Thelwall, Sud and Vis (2012); Sugimoto and Thelwall (2013) "Scholars on soap boxes"; Taleb (2010) on TED as "a monstrosity that turns scientists and thinkers into low-level entertainers". Cited-by: pending. Nothing queued.

### `[Duarte2011]`

Duarte, N. (2011). The secret structure of great talks. TEDxEast (the YouTube upload is dated 11 November 2010; TED.com lists November 2011), 18 minutes. ### `[Conway2014]`

Conway, D. (2014). Instantly Better Presentations. YOW! Nights, Brisbane, 91 minutes. Read in full as the caption transcript (13,947 words), including the audience questions. Evidence: (expert), from a professional speaker whose income is talks, addressed specifically to technical presenters. Seven suggestions: (1) talk only about what you know and love: competence is where confidence comes from, and passion is what keeps a room of primates awake (his Attenborough example); (2) tell it as a story, because stories give flow (the sequence lets the audience infer forgotten details) and hierarchy (three acts of five scenes is fifteen things remembered where a flat list of fifteen cannot be), and any of a historical narrative, a process walkthrough or a war story will do; ### `[Trypke2023]`

### `[Tversky2002]`

Tversky, B., Morrison, J. B., and Bétrancourt, M. (2002). Animation: can it facilitate? *International Journal of Human-Computer Studies* 57(4), 247-262. Read in full (16 pages, Stanford-hosted PDF). Evidence: (meta) as a critical review of the experiments comparing animated with static graphics for teaching complex systems (mechanical, biological, computational), not a statistical meta-analysis. Findings: nearly every study reporting a benefit of animation either gave the animated condition more information than the static one (microsteps, fine-grained actions), or confounded animation with interactivity or with prediction, both known to help on their own (Baek and Layne 1988; Rieber 1990; Park and Gittelman 1992; Thompson and Riding 1990; Kieras 1992; Nathan et al. 1992; Hegarty et al. 1999); properly matched comparisons found no benefit (Morrison and Tversky 2001; Rieber and Hannafin 1988; Rieber 1989, 1990; ChanLin 1998; Pane et al. 1996; Byrne et al. 1999; Payne et al. 1992; Dyck 1995; Harrison 1995), and one found animation-trained students got worse over a week while text-trained students improved (Palmiter and Elkerton 1993).
