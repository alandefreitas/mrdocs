# How to give great talks

A synthesis of what is known about giving talks: what to say, how to structure it, what to put on the screen, how to deliver it. It is organised by claim, not by source. Every claim carries the tag of the source it comes from and a mark for how much evidence stands behind it. It is a living document: each new source read is folded into the section its findings belong to, existing claims gain or lose support, and when a section outgrows its heading the structure is redone. The companion file [HOW-TO-GIVE-GREAT-TALKS-LOG.md](HOW-TO-GIVE-GREAT-TALKS-LOG.md) records each source read, its references, and the reading queue.

## How to read this document

Tags look like `[Author2012]` and resolve in the log. Evidence marks:

- **(meta)**: a meta-analysis or systematic review of experiments, or the same effect replicated across several controlled experiments.
- **(exp)**: at least one controlled experiment with a measured outcome. "Quasi" means groups were not randomised.
- **(obs)**: observational data: surveys, corpus analyses, eye tracking, ratings of real talks, systematic counts of what experts recommend.
- **(expert)**: practitioner advice without measured outcomes, from someone whose talks or teaching earn them a hearing. "Expert consensus" means a recommendation made by at least a fifth of the 91 advice articles in `[Blome2017]`.
- **(own)**: Alan's own notes and practice, including the pattern that won the presentation award at GECCO 2019.
- **(abstract)** after a tag means only the paper's abstract could be read; the claim is limited to what the abstract states.

A claim with several tags has been said by several sources; a claim with one (expert) tag is one person's advice and should be read that way. Where sources disagree, both sides are stated with their marks. Where a popular belief has been tested and failed, it is listed under Myths. Effect sizes are Cohen's d unless stated; above 0.8 is conventionally large.

Three cautions. Most of the experimental evidence comes from laboratory studies of students learning from short narrated animations or lecture slides and tested afterwards, not from conference talks measured for persuasion or memory a week later; the mechanisms (working memory, attention, dual channels) are general, the exact sizes may not carry over. A large share of the slide-design experiments come from one research group (Alley, Garner and colleagues at Penn State and Virginia Tech) and are noted as such. And almost the entire literature on presentation formats failed to randomise *presenters* to formats (100% of 66 studies reviewed in `[Moulton2017]`), so until 2017 nobody had separated the effect of a format from the effect of the kind of person who chooses it; the one study that did is marked.

## 1. What a talk is for

- The speaker's one job is to build an idea in the listeners' minds: a pattern of understanding that was not there before, made only of a voice and a face. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]` What "following the speaker" is, physiologically: in fMRI, a listener's brain activity becomes coupled to the speaker's, the coupling vanishes when communication fails, and the more the listener's activity *anticipates* the speaker's, the better they later score on understanding the story. **(exp; abstract)** `[Stephens2010]`
- The audience is the hero of the talk, not the speaker. The speaker is the mentor who moves them from their ordinary world into the new idea: not Luke Skywalker, Yoda. An idea nobody in the room takes up goes nowhere. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Speaking is learnable. Winston's formula for the quality of a talk is knowledge of technique, times practice, plus a small term for talent: what you know about speaking matters far more than any gift. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Talk only about what you know and, if possible, love. Competence is where confidence comes from: the fear of being caught out by the person at the back disappears when you have actually used the thing. And a speaker who visibly does not care teaches the room not to care; a speaker who is moved by the subject, even in Attenborough's quiet register, is what keeps a room of primates awake. If the topic was assigned, find the part of it that moves you, even if what moves you is how bad it is. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Decide what the talk is doing. Slides suit *exposing* ideas (conference and job talks); a board or a whiteboard suits *teaching* them, because writing speed matches the speed at which people absorb, and no one can absorb at the speed of flipping slides. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Decide what it is for before designing anything: the second most frequent piece of expert advice is to know the audience (53% of advice articles), and 29% say to develop an explicit objective for the talk first. **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]`
- An idea is worth a talk only if someone other than the speaker gains from it. Ask who benefits; if the honest answer is only you or your organisation, the audience will know. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]`
- Slides are asked to do four jobs at once (speaker's notes, the audience's visual, a handout to study later, a substitute for a paper) and fail when one set tries to do all four. Design for the audience in the room; make the handout something else. **(expert, citing Donald Norman)** `[Alley2005]` Write the notes first and hand *those* out; if the slides are doing their job they are useless as a document afterwards. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` One workable order: prepare the full handout deck first, copy it, then strip each copied slide down to two or three words or an image for the screen; the slide deck is the highlight film, the handout is the reading deck. Whether handouts designed for learning help has not been tested; students say they help. **(expert; obs)** `[Berk2012]` If detail is needed for questions, keep it on slides *after* the last slide rather than in the talk. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`
- Know the audience before writing a word: who they are, what they share, how much they already know, whether they care and which facet makes them care (being entertained, instructed, or moved), and what is in it for them. Say what is in it for them. Rename the talk to fit this audience. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]`
- Meaning only attaches to what the audience already knows. Unfamiliar concepts, jargon and symbols will not be understood, or will be understood only with effort the audience makes only if highly motivated; if they are unavoidable, introduce and explain them, and the explanation works better when built from something familiar. **(expert, grounded in the memory literature)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Build the idea piece by piece out of concepts the listeners already have, in their language rather than yours; speakers routinely forget that words they live by mean nothing to the room. Metaphor is the main tool, because it shows how the parts of the new idea fit together in terms of something already understood ("CRISPR is like a word processor for DNA"). **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]` The presenter, being an expert, tends to take for granted concepts the audience does not have; deciding on each slide's assertion is what surfaces them. **(expert)** `[Garner2009]`
- Say what is necessary and nothing else. Less is best, including in length. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Too much information forces the audience to search for the relevant part, which costs effort; the message should carry neither too much nor too little for this audience. **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Speak inclusively: are *we* in this together. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Who you are carries less than you might think. Across 1,202 TED talks, neither the rank of the speaker's university nor their seniority correlated with any popularity measure; being a credentialed academic raised comments and the like ratio, not views. The talk has to do the work. **(obs)** `[Sugimoto2013]` About three quarters of comments on TED talks engage with the content whoever the speaker is; what the speaker's identity changes is how *emotional* the comments about the speaker get (more praise and more criticism for women and for non-academics), not how much the ideas are discussed. **(obs, 5,854 comments coded)** `[Tsou2014]`
- What gets a recording passed on is arousal, not just approval. Across every article the New York Times put online in three months (6,956), the ones that reached the most-emailed list were, controlling for placement, length and author, more positive (+13% odds per standard deviation), more emotional (+18%), and above all more arousing: awe +30%, anger +34%, anxiety +21%, while sadness cut the odds by 16%. Practical usefulness (+30%), interest (+25%) and surprise (+14%) helped too, each about as much as being the lead story for three extra hours. Lab experiments then showed the mechanism: making a story more amusing or more angering raised the urge to share it, and making it sadder lowered it, and in each case the change in physiological arousal carried the effect. **(obs, 6,956 articles; exp, three experiments)** `[Berger2012]` For a talk that will live on YouTube, the moments that make people forward the link are the ones that produce awe or usefulness or surprise, not the calm ones; this is the mechanism behind the emotional-arousal result in Section 2.1 and the reason Jobs's marvelling and the star moment (Section 2.3) work beyond the room.
- Audiences judge the talk and the speaker together. Viewers who rated a presentation as more effective also rated the presenter as more knowledgeable and professional, and those who were persuaded rated both higher (d about 0.8 to 0.9); a better-received talk makes you look better, and vice versa. **(exp)** `[Moulton2017]`

## 2. Structure

### 2.1 Choosing what to say

- One talk carries one idea. Choose the single most important point and make it the last sentence. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Make that idea the throughline: everything in the talk ties back to it, and everything that does not is cut, however good, so that the one thing gets explained properly, with context and examples. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]` A body of work that has too many good ideas leaves people unable to say what it was about; pick the one that sticks out. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Do not start from "what should I say?": people are bad at *recalling* what matters and good at *recognising* it when shown. So first write down everything you *could* say, pages of it, without judging; if you cannot fill pages you should not be giving the talk. Then strike things out until five points remain, because five is about the upper limit of what an audience holds. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- The five must make a story. If the five most important points do not connect, put two back and take two slightly less important ones that do: "five not-quite-as-important points that fit a narrative is better than five incredibly important points that are scattershot". Those five become the chapters; sort the rest of the dump under them or throw it away; a chapter with more than five sub-points gets the same treatment. At most five by five, and the order is already right because the story set it. The slides come last and are the least of the work. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Frame the whole talk as a contrast between *what is* and *what could be*: the status quo, and the world with the idea adopted. Make the gap as large as you can, because the gap is what the audience has to decide about. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Build it as a story with a question, a turn, and a revealed point. Comparing the 153 most-viewed with the 153 least-viewed videos from 52 leading science channels on YouTube, the popular ones more often posed an explicit dramatic question up front, had at least one moment of change (a twist, a "but"), stated their insight outright rather than leaving it implicit, evoked emotional arousal (the largest effect), and were story-driven rather than expository; in a combined model, moderate-or-higher arousal tripled the odds of being in the popular group and a story-driven structure did the same. Point of view (first or third person) and whether the host was on camera made no difference. Correlational, one platform, and extreme-case sampling, but it is the one direct measurement of story components against audience response. **(obs, 306 videos)** `[Huang2020]` The authors' summary of a popular science video: "an emotionally activating journey toward an answer to a science-related question raised at the beginning, with a twist in the middle and a revelation of the gist of the exploration at the end."
- Test of a good talk: a week later, an audience member can name three points, and they are the three you meant. Any three points means the emphasis was wrong; none means the talk failed. For a five-minute talk the number is one. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]` `[Lortie2017]`
- Only talk when you have something to say; the audience's time is not for uninteresting preliminary material. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]`

### 2.2 Opening

- The first thirty seconds decide whether the room is with you. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Open with a story prepared better than anything else in the talk. The story ends at the thing being presented, so the rest of the talk is "what happened next". Rehearse the first two minutes until they are automatic. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Earn the right to build before building. Get the audience's permission by making them curious: a provocative question, a fact that does not fit, a gap pointed out in how they see the world that they will then want filled. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]` Establish what is, then set what could be against it; the contrast is the inciting incident, the moment the audience has to decide whether to come along. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Open with an empowerment promise: tell people what they will know or be able to do at the end that they could not at the start. It is the reason to stay. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Define the topic and give a roadmap at the outset: people organise incoming information into a narrative largely without noticing, and an early outline makes that organisation match yours. **(expert, citing memory research)** `[Kosslyn2012]` **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Make the roadmap visual: a mapping slide with one mnemonic image per section of the talk is remembered far better than a bullet list of section names, because the image is later re-encountered when its section arrives. **(expert, on Paivio's dual coding)** `[Alley2005]` Put an orienting image on the title slide too. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]`
- Three safe ways in: a story told as if to a child ("Once upon a time", "In 2009", "Once, we had this problem"; always about people); a fact that shocks; a question that matters to this audience. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Other openers that work: a direct statement (neutral, provocative, curious, or shocking), a quotation, a captivating visual, a physical object, a relevant joke, an "imagine..." sequence, or a personal experience in less academic settings. Examples of each are in the log under `[Freitas2026]`. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- **Disagreement on opening jokes.** One view: a relevant joke can open a talk, though witty sentences are safer. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` The other: never open with a joke, because in the first minute people are still settling and adjusting to your voice and are not ready to laugh; opening jokes usually fall flat. Save the joke for the end, when they are tuned to you. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Attention grabbers: a large number that makes sense in some light; any physical object; audience interaction ("raise your hand if..."); a contradiction, then a second contradiction. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Show passion for the subject early; people report being inspired by teachers who visibly cared about what they were explaining, and by being shown how cool a result is up front. **(expert, informal survey of students and faculty)** `[Winston2018]` Be enthusiastic. **(expert consensus, 21%)** `[Blome2017]`
- State a questions policy before any content: when questions are welcome (Conway's slides turn blue), and that you are keen to take them. It relaxes the room, because people know when it is all right to ask, and it constrains the one person who would otherwise ask at any moment. Say you are keen even if you plan to take none; it tells the audience their understanding matters. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- In a job talk, the audience decides within about five minutes whether you have a vision (a problem someone cares about plus something new in your approach) and whether you have done something. Establish both before anything else. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Mechanics: stop applause with "Thank you" only if there is applause; catch attention with a flat "Good morning", "Soooo...", or "Alright"; introduce yourself only in academic settings. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Don't apologise, don't open with something irrelevant ("is the mic on?"), don't start negative ("I'm not good at this, but..."), don't repeat what they already know ("My name is ___, this talk is about ___"). Ignore latecomers. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Announcing "I've got a lot to tell you" makes the sympathetic half of the room uncomfortable on your behalf and entertains only the psychopaths. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Audience members volunteer, unprompted, that "a little humor goes a long way". **(obs, free-text survey responses)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Put jokes inside the story only when the story is safe; look for topical humour from the day so nobody hears it twice; keep it relevant. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`

### 2.3 Body

- Tell it as a story, whether or not you say so. Stories were the first information-processing tool humans had, and they do two things a list cannot: *flow* (one thing causes the next, so the audience can re-infer a forgotten detail from the sequence, and memory research suggests we never store details anyway, only the story we rebuild them from) and *hierarchy* (three acts of five scenes is fifteen things remembered, where a flat list of fifteen is not). A historical narrative of the technology, a walkthrough of using it for a task, or a war story of what happened when you did all work; the same material told in a seemingly random order is the commonest fault in technical talks. Isolated problems that fit no thread can be clustered where stories put them, at the end of the second act, provided the third act resolves some of them. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` Stories add to your standing, give the audience anchors, and make you human. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` People sit rapt through a story and flatline through a presentation; the difference is the structure, not the stage. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- The middle moves back and forth between what is and what could be, several times, because audiences resist change and each pass captures some of the resistance, the way a boat tacks against the wind. Make the present unappealing and the future with the idea adopted attractive, alternately. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Tell them what you are going to tell them, then let them know what you can do and prove it with data, then the benefits. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Be logical. **(expert consensus, 23%)** `[Blome2017]`
- Cycle. Say the central idea more than once, going around it from a different side each time. At any given moment a fifth of the audience is fogged out; three passes make it likely everyone catches it. **(expert; the fifth is Winston's estimate)** `[Winston2018]` King's speech used repetition of the same words and phrases so that people could remember and recall them, and the repetition intensified towards the end. **(expert, from mapping the transcript)** `[Duarte2011]` In a short talk, repeat the critical message once, on a later slide, with a different visual (an analogy, a metaphor, a simpler restatement); three times feels repetitive. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]`
- Build a fence around the idea: say explicitly what it is *not*, and how it differs from the nearby idea people will otherwise confuse it with. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Mark the seams. Enumerate ("the first of four things..."), announce the outline, name where you are: this is verbal punctuation, and it gives people who drifted a place to get back on. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Clearly signalled beginnings and ends of sections (a distinctively formatted title or closing slide) help the audience follow. **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Conversely, 64% of real slide decks had no crisp ending to signal that a part, or the whole, was over. **(obs, 140 decks)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Signal the structure of the content. Stressing key words in speech, adding an outline and headings, and showing a map of where you are improved problem-solving transfer (d = 0.74). **(exp, one study)** `[Mayer2003]`
- Vary the medium. Jobs's ninety-minute launch cut from him talking to video, to live demonstration, to a guest speaker, so the audience was never in one mode for long. **(expert, from mapping the talk)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Plant a star moment: something the audience will always remember. When the iPhone's screen scrolled for the first time "you can hear the oxygen sucked out of the room". **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]` The prop and the physical demonstration (Section 3.6) are the usual way to make one. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Model the feeling you want. Jobs marvelled at his own product, out loud and often, more than the audience laughed or clapped; it showed them what to feel. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- Use what the audience already holds sacred. King's speech pulled from scripture, from the songs the crowd had sung together in protest, and from the political promises made to them, so that the picture of the future was painted in their own materials. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]` Metaphor from their world: the "bad check... marked insufficient funds" is a state of affairs everyone has felt. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]` `[Anderson2016]`
- Design every slide so that a person who heard nothing you said still gets its main point: attention lapses are normal in a long session, and the slide is what catches the lapsed. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`
- State the assertions and the assumptions behind them explicitly. Audiences are more inclined to believe an argument when they can see its claims and its warrants (Toulmin); a talk that only lists topics never shows either. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]`
- Ask the room a question and wait. Seven seconds of silence feels like an eternity to the speaker and is the normal time an answer takes. The question must be neither so obvious that answering embarrasses people nor so hard that nobody has anything to say. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Segment. When the material is dense and the pace is fast, splitting it into bite-size pieces with a pause between them let learners finish organising one piece before the next arrived (d = 1.36). **(exp, one study)** `[Mayer2003]` Working memory holds only about four units at a time, so a new piece that arrives before the last one is filed simply displaces it. **(expert, on the memory literature)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Plan a pause. Stopping speaking for a moment over the one complex slide lets the audience catch their breath and absorb it; expect them to do that many times and they tune out. Showing a finding and saying little is also a way to signal that it matters. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]`
- Pre-train. Introducing the names and behaviour of the parts before explaining how the system works improved transfer (d = 1.00) by letting the audience build the component model first and the causal model second. **(exp, three studies)** `[Mayer2003]`
- Situate. Put the work in context: what problem, who else is trying, why it matters. Failure to situate is, with failure to practise, the usual reason oral exams go badly. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- To be remembered, give the work a symbol, a slogan (a phrase that is a handle for it), a surprise, a salient idea (one that sticks out, not necessarily the most important), and a story of how it was done, how it works and why it matters. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- When something breaks, tell a story. Jobs's clicker died mid-launch and he filled the gap with a personal anecdote rather than dead air. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`

### 2.4 Closing

- State the key idea again, as what you want them to believe or as a call to action. Tell them what you have told them. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Summarise at the end. **(expert consensus, 26%)** `[Blome2017]`
- Give a call to action and next steps. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Every talk should have one, and it belongs at the very end. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- End on the new bliss: describe the world with the idea adopted, and do it poetically and dramatically, not as a summary. King's "free at last"; Jobs's Gretzky line about skating to where the puck is going to be. **(expert)** `[Duarte2011]`
- End with a bang and go full circle back to the opening metaphor or story. The final sentence is the single most important point, stated in an unexpected or catchy way. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Repeat one or two key images from the talk on the conclusion slide; the image seen twice anchors the point. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]`
- Give the ending room. Begin closing the story arc several slides before the end; an abrupt stop jars, and the audience, which has been matching your pace, reads it as the end of a dialogue rather than of a talk. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]` Set the stage, tell the story, have a big finish where the take-home message is clearly understood. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]`
- The conclusion slide is the most important slide of the talk, and it should stay up through the questions, so it is the one people remember. It states the contributions or the conclusions. Not the list of collaborators (put those on the first slide), not "Questions?", not "Thank you", not a bare contact address: all of those waste the most-seen slide in the talk. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` `[Alley2005]` An explicit, distinctively formatted ending is one of the things audiences most often find missing. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` **Exception for formats with no question period:** in a lightning or rapid-fire talk the last slide is the only chance to give contact details and a link to the deck, so there it should carry them. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]` Acknowledge contributors at the start or at the point of their contribution, not in a rushed list at the end. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]`
- **Disagreement on "thank you".** One view: signal that you have finished ("That's all I have", "I rest my case"), then thank them. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` The other: do not make "thank you" the last words; it suggests the audience stayed out of politeness. Mouth it once the applause starts if you like, but end on something else: a joke (they are tuned to you by now; "finish with a joke and people think they had fun the whole time"), a benediction, or a salute to the audience saying what you valued about being with them. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`

## 3. Slides

### 3.1 Words on the screen versus words in the air

- Slides are for looking at, not reading. One image, or one word. If the audience can read the slide they stop listening. The content of the talk lives in the speaker's notes and comes out of the speaker's mouth. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Slides are condiments to what you say, not the main course. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` A presentation is an exercise in listening, not reading; do what you can to let them listen. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` Whatever is on screen should be the slogan of the moment: short enough to be read in a glance and then ignored. Apple's slides are one or two words with a four- or five-word subtext, readable in a tenth of a second so the audience can go back to listening; that, not the back row, is why they are so sparse. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` Put the text you were going to show into the notes pane and speak it. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`
- The mechanism: words and pictures are processed in two separate channels, each of limited capacity. Text on a slide competes for the *visual* channel with the picture on the slide, while speech uses the auditory channel that is otherwise idle. **(meta, theory supported by dozens of experiments)** `[Mayer2003]` Winston's version: we have one language processor, and it can read or listen but not both, so words on the slide force the audience to read instead of listen. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` `[Naegle2021]` The same account, in the cognitive-load vocabulary: many words on screen plus speech overload the verbal track; replacing the words with images moves the load to the otherwise idle pictorial track. **(expert, on the same literature)** `[Garner2009]`
- So, when there is a picture on screen, explaining it in speech beats explaining it in on-screen text: the modality effect, median d = 1.17 across six experiments. **(meta)** `[Mayer2003]`
- **Text that duplicates the speech, next to a picture, hurts.** Full sentences on screen that repeat the narration while a picture is also showing impaired retention and transfer in the original experiments (median d = 0.69) **(meta, three experiments)** `[Mayer2003]` and in the later replications; across a systematic review of 63 randomised studies, adding *identical* text to a narrated visual is the configuration most consistently harmful. **(meta)** `[Trypke2023]`
- **A few words that summarise the speech, next to a picture, help or do no harm.** The same review finds that short, abridged or phrase-level text alongside narration and a picture improved retention or transfer, or was neutral, where identical full text hurt: keywords improved retention though not transfer, abridged text beat no text on transfer, and phrase-level text beat both full text and bare keywords in one study. A meta-analysis of the verbal-redundancy literature finds the same: narration plus short written keywords aids learning, narration plus identical text impairs it. **(meta)** `[Trypke2023, reporting Mayer2008, Yue2013, Sombatteera2012, Adesope2012]` So the one-word or one-line slide is not merely harmless: it is the form of on-screen text that the evidence favours. The words act as signals that mark what matters.
- **With no picture at all, text and speech together beat speech alone.** In the studies that added written text to narration with nothing else on screen, the combination generally improved learning, most when the text was short, the content complex, and the listeners had low prior knowledge, low working-memory capacity, or were older; identical long text under a fixed pace was the exception that hurt. The written words compensate for speech being transient. **(meta)** `[Trypke2023]` **(exp, two experiments)** `[Mayer2003]` A single word or line on a bare background reinforces speech; text next to a diagram fights it.
- **For an expert audience, do not narrate what a self-explanatory picture already shows.** Narrating a diagram helped novices and *hurt* more knowledgeable learners in the same studies (the expertise-reversal effect); duplicated information that a novice needs is redundant load for an expert. **(exp, four experiments, second-hand)** `[Trypke2023, reporting Kalyuga2000, Leslie2012]` A conference room is an expert audience: say what the picture does not, not what it does.
- When slides are heavy with text, people remember what was on the slide rather than what was said, and resent the speaking: one participant in an informal experiment wrote "I wish you hadn't talked so much, it was distracting". **(expert; anecdote of a student's uncontrolled experiment)** `[Winston2018]` Where slide text and narration compete, eye tracking shows viewers cut their attention to the text rather than to the picture, a strategy for coping with redundancy. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Garner2013, citing Wiebe2008]` A wall of text severs the connection between speaker and room: "there's no point in saying anything until you've read through this completely unnecessary essay on what I'm about to say"; bullets are not the fairy dust that fixes it. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- "Speakers read word-for-word from notes or from the slides themselves" is one of the three problems audiences report most often and find most annoying. **(obs, survey of 205 regular presentation attendees)** `[Kosslyn2012]` People know how to read; reading the slide to them annoys them. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Do not read the talk from slides or a manuscript. **(expert consensus, 44%)** `[Blome2017]` The more text a slide holds, the more tempting it is to read it aloud, which is one more reason to keep it off. **(expert)** `[Garner2009]`
- Take off the title, the logos, and the background decoration. You are telling them the title; it does not need to be on the slide. Everything removed lets them attend to you. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Strip the corporate background, the slide number ("a death march"), your name and the talk title from every slide, the bar along the bottom, and above all the logo: it is the one thing consistent from slide to slide, so it is what draws the eye, and what it says is "salesperson". Keep those on the first and last slide only. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` Shrink the title box: PowerPoint templates make the title the largest element and the content the smallest, the reverse of where attention should go. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`

### 3.2 If a slide has a headline, make it the point in one sentence

This is the *assertion-evidence* design: a full-sentence headline stating the slide's claim, supported by visual evidence, no bullet list. It is the best-tested slide structure in the literature, though most of the tests come from the group that invented it.

- Audiences learn more from it. In a randomised experiment (110 engineering students, identical recorded narration, only the slides differed), the assertion-evidence group wrote better essays on how the system worked (d = 0.81), recalled the causal sequence better (d = 0.60), held fewer misconceptions (d = 0.47), answered more inference questions correctly right afterwards, and scored higher on an unannounced test ten days later (d = 0.89). They also reported lower mental effort (d = 0.50). Recall of isolated facts did not differ. **(exp, one randomised study)** `[Garner2013]`
- In a large course taught live (four sections, about 740 students), rewriting phrase headlines as sentence headlines raised recall of the headline's assertion on the exam from 69% to 79% (p < .001), with gains on seven of fifteen questions and losses on two, even though the sentence-headline sections put measurably less effort into the course. **(exp, quasi-experimental: intact sections, live lectures)** `[Alley2006]` Replacing a phrase-headline lecture with sentence headlines, arrows on the graphics and sparing text also raised medical students' scores on a clinical topic. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Garner2013, citing Issa2011]`
- A question as headline did worse than a sentence: 24% recall against 58% for the same slide, because the audience had to assemble the answer from fragments in the body. A slide with no headline at all did worst (23% against 57%). **(exp, quasi, single slides)** `[Alley2006]`
- Why it works, in the terms of the rest of this section: the sentence headline signals the point (signalling), it forces the body to carry only what supports that point (coherence), it is a one-sentence summary of some ten spoken sentences rather than a transcript of them (so it is the abridged, not the identical, kind of on-screen text), and it pushes the presenter to find an image that *explains* rather than decorates. **(expert, on the multimedia literature)** `[Garner2009]` `[Garner2013]` Independently arrived at: use the heading to state the message ("CTNND1 is central to metastasis", not "Results"), so that the heading works as the topic sentence of a paragraph whose body is the slide. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` A short full-sentence assertion heading "will increase students' memory of the point of the slide"; and the single word on a slide, followed by a picture, is named as an appropriate alternative for some content. **(expert, reviewing the literature)** `[Berk2012]`
- **Disagreement on sentences versus phrases.** The expert-advice literature leans the other way: 24% of advice articles say to put phrases, not sentences, on slides **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]`, two recent guides call sentences on slides a mistake **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` `[Phillips2014]`, and the redundancy evidence above favours phrases over full text. The experiments in this section are about *one* sentence, the headline, over a visual body; the advice is about bodies full of sentences that duplicate the speech. Both can be right: one sentence stating the claim, none anywhere else, and that sentence a summary of the speech rather than a transcript of it.
- The guidelines that go with it: a sentence headline of no more than two lines, left-justified at the top left; visual evidence in the body (images, graphs, or words arranged visually with arrows); no bullet lists, because they hide the connections between the listed items; text blocks of one or two lines; lists, if unavoidable, of two to four items; internal white space between elements rather than a border of it; at least a minute per slide, more in a long talk. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]` Bullets in particular flatten hierarchy: the first bullet reads as no more important than the last. **(expert, citing Shaw et al. 1998)** `[Alley2006]`
- Writing the sentence is hard, and that is the point: to write it the presenter has to know what the slide is for, which is why experienced presenters used to phrase headlines find the design an unexpected effort and why it cannot be done the hour before. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]` A materials-science professor who converted a five-institution course: "When I create a sentence headline, I think about what main assertion I want the students to remember from that slide. Just that act makes my lectures more focused." **(expert, anecdote)** `[Garner2009]`
- Once people learn it, they keep using it: three months or more after a workshop, 67% of 62 professionals and graduate students still used sentence headlines on all or most slides and 82% still used visual evidence; 57% reported their audiences mostly receptive and 4% averse. **(obs)** `[Alley2005]`
- How far practice is from this: in a sample of over 2,000 slides from industry professionals, conference presenters and international PhD students, 84% to 94% of slides had a phrase headline, 63% to 75% had a bullet list, the average slide carried eight lines and 33 to 43 words, about 40% had no graphic at all, and of the graphics present roughly half only *represented* the topic and a third *explained* anything. **(obs)** `[Garner2009]`

### 3.3 How much per slide

- One slide, one message. It is the one rule every practitioner in this document states independently **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` `[Conway2014]` `[Phillips2014]`, it follows from the four-unit limit of working memory **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]`, and the way to honour it is more slides, not more per slide: Conway's deck had 277, Phillips's grew from 95 to 135, and Phillips's line is that the number of slides was never the problem, only the number of topics on each. Where a company limits the slide count, people cram forty slides' content into four. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` `[Phillips2014]` Break a complex diagram into a sequence of slides that builds it up, one part per slide. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Style, for a technical talk, is "the things you don't see that make sure the things you do see are the things you should see": anything on the slide that is not the message distracts from it, and that includes extra fonts, colours and decoration. Content is a small payload; style is the delivery system, and the payload does not arrive on a goat cart. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Treat a slide like a figure in a paper: one message, no superfluous elements, and do not paste in a figure made for print, because the audience has no time to parse it or read rotated labels. When a data figure matters, give it a whole slide with the eye directed to the one attribute that carries the point, and put the interpretation on the next slide as a single sentence. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]`
- There are always too many slides and always too many words: Winston's diagnosis of every deck he was ever asked to critique, made before seeing it. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Keep slides simple is the single most frequent piece of expert advice (63% of articles), with limits on lines per slide (43%) and words per line (29%) close behind. **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]`
- **But cutting text by itself has weak support.** The three retention experiments the systematic reviewers could find that compared concise with detailed slides found no difference in what students remembered. **(exp, three studies, second-hand)** `[Blome2017, citing Wecker2012, Katt2008, Blokzijl2005]` What *did* move learning in the experiments in this document was not fewer words but a different slide: a stated claim over an explaining picture `[Garner2013]`, speech instead of text next to a picture `[Mayer2003]`, a few signal words instead of a transcript `[Trypke2023]`, and the removal of material that is off the point `[Mayer2003]`. Less text is a side effect of those, not the lever.
- About a minute per slide: a twenty-minute talk has around twenty slides; if a slide takes longer than a minute in rehearsal, it holds more than one idea. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` More than one visual per minute of talking is too many and you will run over. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]` Where experts specified a slide count they most often said one per minute. **(expert consensus, 27% say "not too many slides")** `[Blome2017]` At least a minute per slide, more in a long talk. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]` A one-word slide can be on screen for seconds, so this is a ceiling on content per slide, not a floor on time. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- "The slides contained too much material to absorb before the next slide was presented" is one of the three most prevalent and most annoying problems reported by audiences. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Every one of 140 real decks sampled from the web violated at least one limited-capacity rule. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Arithmetic of a conference slide: about a minute on screen, 33 words on it, and a speaker talking at 120 words a minute. A minute is not enough to read and comprehend the 33 words and also listen to the 120. **(obs for the averages; expert for the conclusion)** `[Garner2009]`
- Working memory holds about four perceptual units; hierarchical grouping into groups of no more than four extends that. Rules that follow: no more than four items in a list, no more than two lines per item, nothing on a slide that cannot be read aloud in about a minute. **(expert, on the memory literature)** `[Kosslyn2012]` `[Conway2014]` These are also the rules most often broken in practice: lists not built one item at a time (96% of decks), more than two lines per item (91%), more than four items (91%), no hierarchy (86%). **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` A popular talk claims six elements as the limit beyond which processing cost jumps; the claim rests on a live counting demonstration, not a study, and the subitizing it demonstrates tops out nearer four. **(expert, unsupported)** `[Phillips2014]` `[Naegle2021]`
- Images, and one word at a time. Never more than four bullet points on screen, and ideally none. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Everything on the slide must be something you intend to talk about; anything else is a shiny bauble that the audience, and especially senior people in it, will chase instead of following you. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` A second message on the slide is like hearing your name at a party: attention goes to it involuntarily and what the speaker said meanwhile is gone. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`
- The heaviness test: print the deck and lay it out on a table. Too much text, not enough air, not enough imagery is visible at a glance. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Cut everything interesting but extraneous. Background music, decorative clips and tangents primed learners to process the irrelevant material and hurt transfer: the coherence effect, median d = 0.90 across five experiments. **(meta)** `[Mayer2003]` "The main point was obscured by lots of irrelevant detail" is one of the three complaints audiences make most often. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Sound effects have no place unless the sound *is* the message (a cantata, the noise a failing drive makes). **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` The one qualification from the redundancy literature: irrelevant material that is easy to ignore does little harm to experienced learners, who learn to skip it; novices cannot. **(meta)** `[Trypke2023]`
- Audiences have a sense of "about right". Viewers who judged a presentation to have too much *or* too little text, or too few graphs, images or animations, rated it and its presenter worse and were less persuaded by it; most judged the amount of text about right and were more critical of missing visuals than of missing words. **(exp, 1,069 viewers)** `[Moulton2017]` Too minimal reads as a sales pitch. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- One deliberately impossible slide is allowed per talk, and exactly one: a diagram so complex the audience cannot understand it, shown to make the point that the thing is complex. Winston calls it a hapax legomenon. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Building a list one item at a time is what audiences say they prefer **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012, citing Apperson2008]`, and steering the eye through a list by dimming everything but the current item is a practitioner staple **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`, but one study found it improved memory for only two of nine items compared with showing the list at once, so the memory benefit is small and unproven. **(exp, one study, second-hand)** `[Kosslyn2012, citing Mahar2009]` Experts disagree among themselves about whether to reveal a slide sequentially. **(obs)** `[Blome2017]`

### 3.4 Pictures, diagrams and motion

- Connect consecutive slides with motion (transitions, magic-move animations) so the picture on screen keeps relating to what is being said. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` This has the strongest experimental support of anything about slides in this document, with one qualification about what kind of motion. In the only study to randomise presenters to formats (146 presenters, 45 minutes to prepare a five-minute pitch, judged live by 153 viewers and on video by 1,069 more), presentations built on a single canvas that the presenter panned and zoomed across, so that the motion showed how the parts related, were rated more organised, engaging, persuasive and effective than slide decks and than talks with no visuals, by about half a point on a five-point scale; their presenters were rated more knowledgeable and professional; and they shifted the audience's business decision by 16 points against 12 for slides and 8 for no visuals. Slide decks and no visuals were rated the same on every dimension. The authors attribute the effect to the panning-and-zooming animation, which viewers named as the attribute slide decks most lacked, not to the software. **(exp, two randomised experiments)** `[Moulton2017]`
- Show the picture at the moment the words about it are spoken. Presenting a picture and its narration at the same time rather than one after the other improved transfer with median d = 1.30 across eight experiments: the largest, most replicated effect in this literature. The effect disappears when the successive pieces are each only a few seconds long. **(meta)** `[Mayer2003]`
- **Showing code, data or any text-shaped thing.** Never a block of it with a laser pointer and five minutes of talk; by the second such slide the first is gone from memory and nothing correlates. Instead: build the diagram step by step so the audience watches the structure form; when walking through code, copy the slide as many times as there are things to say and brighten only the lines you are talking about, dimming the rest, so the eye is always exactly where your voice is (it also stops you from ever losing your place, because whatever is bright is what you say next); show a refactoring as a run of slides that each change one thing, which "took thirty seconds to produce" and is the clearest refactoring anyone has seen; show a transformation, such as procedural to functional code, by morphing one form into the other so the relationship stays visible. The same works for XML, file formats, tables and graphs. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Not all pictures help. Images that *represent* the content improve recall; decorative images (template art, clip art, stock photos that illustrate the mood) reduce it. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Alley2005, citing Carney2002]` A picture that shows only one of three bullets, or that mirrors the text, is a partial representation and does little. **(obs; expert)** `[Garner2009]` Images incongruent with the text depressed test scores below text-only slides. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Garner2013, citing Tangen2011]` Irrelevant pictures hurt recall in the one classroom study of them. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Blome2017, citing Bartsch2003]` Almost never show a slide that is only text; build slides around a visualisation and introduce each element of a data figure aloud. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` Pictures are like salt: a little improves everything, and a talk of nothing but pictures works only for the very few who can find images that carry the point as well as a couple of words would; a subtle, peripherally related picture sets the audience puzzling over what it is for. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- **Animation that explains a process has no proven advantage over a well-made sequence of stills, and can do harm.** A review of the experiments comparing animated with static graphics for teaching how systems work found that nearly every apparent win for animation came from the animation carrying more information than the static version, or from interactivity or prediction that was confounded with it; matched comparisons found no benefit, and some found text or stills retained better a week later. The reasons: motion is often too fast or too complex to perceive accurately (people misdraw the paths of pendulums and falling objects even after watching them), people conceive of continuous change as a sequence of discrete steps anyway, a series of frames can be compared and re-inspected while an animation is fleeting, and novices watching an animation attend to whatever moves most rather than to what matters. What does work: schematic rather than realistic, annotated with arrows or highlighting, broken into discrete steps at the audience's pace, with zooming and changes of viewpoint available; and real-time reorientation in space is the one use the review calls promising. **(meta, review of experiments)** `[Tversky2002]` A presenter clicking through a build, one change per slide, with the shared elements held in place, is exactly that discrete, paced, annotated form; a video of the process playing on its own is the form the review warns against.
- The two kinds of motion. Movement is the strongest attention cue there is; animation captures and directs attention. That is useful when the movement points at what matters and harmful when it does not: gratuitous animation that obscures rather than illuminates appeared in 12% of decks. **(expert, on attention research; obs for the prevalence)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Flying bullets, fly-ins and flashing text are disliked in every survey and are the animations meant when experts say to avoid animation. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` Slide transitions and object entrances and exits are "seductive details" of the kind shown to hurt learning; zooming and panning over one canvas to show how things connect is the opposite kind of motion, and is what the audiences above preferred. **(exp for the preference; expert for the distinction)** `[Moulton2017]` The panning and zooming that audiences preferred is the "reorientation in space" that the animation review singles out as the use that has stood up. **(meta)** `[Tversky2002]` The eye goes involuntarily to what moves, to strong colour, to contrast and to size; that is a lever, and it must point at the message. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]` Use animations carefully. **(expert consensus, 28%)** `[Blome2017]`
- Put labels on the thing, not in a key. Words far from the part of the picture they describe force visual search; integrating text into the graphic improved transfer (d = 0.48). **(exp, one study)** `[Mayer2003]` Eliminating the need to search for labels reduces load. **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Short text integrated into a complex picture works as a signal and can help even an expert audience; text a novice needs to follow a complex picture should be short and placed in the picture. **(meta)** `[Trypke2023]`
- Point with the slide, not with a laser.
- **Disagreement on animated visuals.** One synthesis for teachers states that animated visuals and infographics "can enhance learning significantly more than static visuals", and endorses letter, word and graphic animation and slide transitions used systematically. **(expert, citing the author's own reviews)** `[Berk2012]` The controlled comparisons above say otherwise for explanatory animation, and the same author warns that movement, music and sound effects are seductive details that can harm learning. The claim is recorded here as contested and the controlled evidence is preferred. A laser pointer turns your head to the screen and breaks contact with the room ("we could all leave and he wouldn't know"); put a numbered arrow on the image and say "look at the end of arrow one". **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Laser pointers "take an audience of primates and turn them into felines"; highlight on the slide instead. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` Experts disagree among themselves about pointers. **(obs)** `[Blome2017]`
- Pictures are often ambiguous; when they are, a label makes them clear. Choose the graphic form by the point: line graphs for trends, bar graphs for specific values, charts for structure or sequence, maps for territory. **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Use simple tables and graphs. **(expert consensus, 34%)** `[Blome2017]` A large table becomes readable when everything but the row under discussion is greyed. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`
- If the most salient thing on the slide is also the most important, the audience attends to it and remembers it better later. In practice the most important element was not the most salient in about a third of decks. **(expert for the mechanism; obs for the prevalence)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Make the important thing the biggest thing. **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]`

### 3.5 Typography, colour and consistency

- Type must be large enough to read: words not larger than about 20 point were unreadable in 66% of decks and in 70% of research decks specifically. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Readable slides are the sixth most frequent expert recommendation (43%); where a size was named it ranged from 18 to 32 points, 24 most often. **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]` The assertion-evidence guideline is 28 point for the headline and 18 to 24 for body text, bold sans serif in a room of more than twenty people, and it was chosen by sitting in the back row and the sharpest-angled front seats to see what stayed readable. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]` `[Alley2006]` Winston's floor is far higher, around 40 to 50 point; the point of a large minimum is not legibility but that it physically prevents cramming words onto the slide. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` A teaching synthesis puts the minimum at 30 point for body text, larger for headings, in a clean sans serif, and says to check every word from the back of the room. **(expert)** `[Berk2012]`
- Avoid all-caps, all-italic or all-bold runs (hard to discriminate; found in 81% of decks), underlining, deep saturated blue for text, and red next to blue (the eye cannot focus both at once, and the boundary shimmers). Warm colours read as foreground, cool colours as background. **(expert, on vision research; obs for prevalence)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Avoid text set in all capitals. **(expert)** `[Alley2005]` Sans serif, large, bold for emphasis rather than italics or underline, high contrast, plain backgrounds, and palettes that survive colour blindness: the same rules that help everyone are the ones that make the slide usable for people with dyslexia or low vision. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]` Use contrasting colours. **(expert consensus, 21%)** `[Blome2017]` Graphic designers' rule, endorsed for slides: a cool background (blues, greens) that recedes, with warm type (yellow, orange, red) that advances and draws the eye; colours shift on projectors, so check them in rehearsal. **(expert)** `[Berk2012]` `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Audiences assume that any change in appearance carries information. Random changes in background, bullet symbols, colour or typeface between slides mislead; 72% of decks changed visual properties that signalled nothing. Keep a consistent look and change it only to mean something. **(expert; obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Consistent design throughout. **(expert consensus, 21%)** `[Blome2017]`
- **Light or dark background is the one thing experts contradict each other on.** 26% of advice articles say dark, 3% say light, and the reviewers note the dark camp gives no reasons while the light camp says slides are easier to read; they add that dark backgrounds may force dimmed lights, and dimmed lights make people tired. **(obs; expert)** `[Blome2017]` One reason for dark that is at least an argument: a bright white screen is the largest, highest-contrast object in the room and it wins the eye against the speaker; a dark screen lets the speaker be the brightest thing, and "I am the presentation, this is my visual aid". **(expert)** `[Phillips2014]` Avoid dark colours when slides will be printed or read on paper; for a projected or streamed talk, match the venue's palette instead. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` See also "keep the lights up" under Preparation.
- Slides that fade in or out slowly let the audience organise the material wrongly before it is complete and then re-organise it, which costs effort. **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- The software's defaults are the enemy. PowerPoint's master slide (a short centred title box over a large bulleted text box that shrinks the type as you add words) has barely changed since the 1980s and is what produces the phrase-headline-plus-bullets slide; every deviation from it costs keystrokes, so most people never deviate. Start from a template that does not have that shape. **(obs for the prevalence; expert for the remedy)** `[Garner2009]` `[Garner2013]` `[Alley2005]` `[Phillips2014]`

### 3.6 Boards, props and the physical world

- A prop is the thing people remember twenty years later. Playwrights know this (Ibsen's stove in *Hedda Gabler*); so do the best lecturers (a bicycle wheel, a pendulum released at the lecturer's own nose). Winston's speculation is that watching a physical demonstration engages "empathic mirroring" in a way a picture cannot. **(expert; the mechanism is his own conjecture)** `[Winston2018]`
- Any physical object captures attention. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- A board gives the hands a job, lets you point at what you are discussing, and paces the talk at writing speed. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Whiteboard-style animation was preferred to a slideware version of the same online lecture, matched for words and pictures. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Moulton2017, citing Turkay2016]`

## 4. Delivery

- Energy transfers. An audience of primates responds to a speaker the way a troop responds to the one member who comes in agitated: they match it. Calm, flat delivery is read as "the presenter is bored by this, why should I be interested". The energy does not have to be loud (Attenborough's is quiet) but it has to be real, which is why passion for the subject is a delivery technique and not only a preparation one. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Make eye contact. It is the third most frequent expert recommendation (46% of articles) **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]`, and the one delivery habit with an experiment behind it: students shown a presenter who kept eye contact, spoke with life and did not read from a text rated the talk more credible and comprehensible and scored higher on a later test, though the three were varied together. **(exp, one study, second-hand)** `[Blome2017, citing Blokzijl2005]` Look at one person at a time and change at the pauses. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Face the audience. **(expert consensus, 23%)** `[Blome2017]` Stand near the screen. A speaker far from the slides makes the audience swing between the two like a tennis match. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Viewers who preferred a talk with no slides at all said it was because of the eye contact and the undivided attention they could give the speaker. **(obs, free responses)** `[Moulton2017]`
- Vary the voice instead of speaking in a monotone (30%); do not speak too fast (24%). **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]` Articulate clearly. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Rehearsing with automatic captions on shows where pace, volume and articulation lose words. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`
- Leave the arms free to move; embody the characters in the story. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Novices become aware of their hands and hide them in pockets or behind the back, which reads badly (in some cultures as insulting: it is where a weapon would be). Give the hands something to do: point, write, hold the prop. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Pause instead of filling ("mmm", "well...", "so..."). Wait in silence while thinking. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Treat the floor as a stage: be entertaining, but within your limits. If you are not humorous by nature do not try to be; if you cannot tell an anecdote do not tell one. A good entertainer is more likely to be remembered, a bad attempt is not. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]` Whatever the room, address the "why" and let the pleasure in the subject show. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]`
- Don't judge; be positive. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Don't read the slides or the notes aloud: it is the single most-cited annoyance in audience surveys. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` **(expert consensus, 44%)** `[Blome2017]` **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` `[Garner2009]`

## 5. Questions and answers

- Be prepared for questions. **(expert consensus, 21%)** `[Blome2017]` Keep the detail you cut from the talk on slides after the conclusion, to pull up when asked. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`
- When a question or a discussion starts, black the screen (the B key in most slide software) so the room looks at people, not at the slide. **(expert)** `[Berk2012]`
- Questions are essential to the audience, because you cannot know which parts they misheard or misread, and even the people who do not get to ask leave happier having seen questions answered. They are a nuisance to the speaker only when they arrive unscheduled; the fix is the policy announced at the start (Section 2.2): questions at the end of each chapter, or in a reserved ten minutes, or in the corridor afterwards. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` Leave Q&A to the end, or people interrupt the flow. Ask for questions that relate to the talk. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Don't let one person ask many questions or make long comments; interrupt if needed. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` The persistent questioner wants to be seen as your intellectual peer, not to have the question answered. Answer the first briefly and turn to someone else; on the second, say it is a fascinating question at a level above where the room is and offer to discuss it privately afterwards, which gives them exactly what they wanted and buys silence. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Listen to the whole question; wait until it is clear before answering; repeat it for the room if it was asked without a microphone. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Repeat *every* question, rephrased, because you are the only person in the room everyone can hear, and the rephrasing checks you understood it. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Don't rush to say "great question": it says the others weren't. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Support answers with facts and figures; preface opinion with "in my opinion"; say so when you don't know. Punctuate answers with a story. Keep eye contact with the questioner. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` "I don't know" raises credibility; a made-up answer is always detected and destroys it. After about the third "I don't know" in a row, say plainly that this is an area you are not expert in and move on; the room prefers that too. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- If body language says the answer wasn't understood, ask whether they have a follow-up. Don't think aloud; don't be verbose. Don't end abruptly: "There's time for two more questions." **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Deferring a question to afterwards is a legitimate tool for keeping the room in flow. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Whatever is on screen during the questions is the slide seen longest; make it the conclusions, not "Questions?". **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` `[Alley2005]`

## 6. Preparation, rehearsal and the room

### 6.1 Building the talk

- Writing the slides as assertions makes you understand the material. Students who built each slide as a full-sentence claim supported by a picture scored 12.6 out of 20 on an unannounced essay test the next day against 10.7 for students who built the usual topic-phrase-plus-bullets slides (p = 0.01), with the largest gap on the hardest step of the process and fewer misconceptions. Composing the sentence forces you to decide what the slide is *for*. **(exp, one study, about 50 engineering students per group)** `[Aippersbach2013]` The same effect reported from the other side of the lectern: writing the sentence headline "makes my lectures more focused". **(expert, anecdote)** `[Garner2009]` `[Alley2005]`
- Write the content (the brain dump, the five points, the story) before opening the slide software; the slides are the last and least step, and a dump written once is a resource for every later talk on the subject. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Know the topic "like the back of your hand". **(expert consensus, 32%)** `[Blome2017]` If you were sent to talk about something you have not used, your first job is to go and use it. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Review the slides against a checklist of perception and memory principles before presenting; people do not spot these flaws by instinct (see Myths). **(expert, following their own experiment)** `[Kosslyn2012]`

### 6.2 Rehearsal

- Rehearse out loud, for duration, as a full recital, more than once. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]` Rehearsal is the fourth most frequent expert recommendation (44%), rehearsal in front of other people is recommended by a third, timing the talk beforehand by 39%, and sticking to the allotted time by 41%. **(expert consensus)** `[Blome2017]` Audiences ask for exactly this, unprompted: speakers should rehearse "so they don't look like it's the first time they're seeing these slides too". **(obs, free-text responses)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- Never give a talk for the first time. Before any audience that matters, give it live at least three times: standing, out loud, in the real time slot, to some audience, and colleagues over lunch, a partner, a cat, or a photograph of a suitable crowd on a large monitor all count. What it trains is the experience of speaking aloud to faces. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` An important talk is never given for the first time to an audience of peers; give it first to collaborators, who will point out the discrepancies more gently. Take every chance to present (journal club, teaching), because the more you give the better you get. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]`
- Record yourself. Nothing shows the violations of every other rule like listening to or watching a recording of your own talk; seeing what is wrong is easy, and correcting it next time is not. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]` `[Lortie2017]` Rehearse standing, out loud, several times, without notes, with planned pauses, and for an auto-advancing format a few seconds short per slide to absorb lag and laughter. **(expert)** `[Lortie2017]`
- Stick to what you practised. Deviating on the day, and above all starting on material you know less well than the audience, is the common failure; the more you practise the less likely you are to wander, and visual cues on the slides help keep you on the path. **(expert)** `[Bourne2007]`
- Rehearse in front of people who do *not* know the work. People who know it hallucinate material that is not in the talk; your supervisor is the worst possible debugger of your talk for that reason. Tell the friends that if they cannot make you cry you will not value them as friends. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` Test the talk on trusted friends specifically to find the places where they get confused. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]`
- In rehearsal, the things that most often need fixing are slides that take longer than a minute (too much on them), non-essential details, and forgotten credits; the transitions between slides, what you say as one ends and the next begins, are what keep the story flowing and are what practice reveals. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`

### 6.3 Nerves

- **Relabel the fear as excitement. This is the best-supported piece of delivery advice in the document.** In four experiments, people about to sing, speak or sit a timed test were assigned to say "I am excited", "I am calm", "I am anxious", or nothing. Before a two-to-three-minute persuasive speech on camera, those who said "I am excited" rather than "I am calm" were rated by blind judges as more persuasive (d = 0.68), more competent (d = 0.46), more confident (d = 0.71) and more persistent (d = 0.51), and spoke longer (167 against 132 seconds, d = 1.48); in karaoke, "I am excited" scored 81% against 69% for saying nothing and 53% for "I am anxious"; in the maths test the instruction "try to get excited" beat "try to remain calm" (d = 0.36). Heart rate stayed high in every condition, including "try to remain calm": the arousal does not go down, excitement is added on top of it, and the mechanism is a shift from seeing the task as a threat to seeing it as an opportunity. **(exp, four experiments, 659 participants)** `[Brooks2014]` The practitioner's version: the sensations of dread before a talk and of excitement before a first date are the same; which you feel is the word you attach to it, so say "I am energised" every time, and expect it to take repetition. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]` The fear itself is a reasonable response to a strange situation: one primate facing the wrong way while every other primate stares at it and then bares its teeth. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- Do the relabelling right before, not days before. Saying "I am excited" immediately before the task helped; the author speculates that anxiety felt a week out may usefully drive preparation. **(exp; expert for the speculation)** `[Brooks2014]`
- The other remedy is the one above: having given the talk three times already, it is not the first time. **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`

### 6.4 The room and the equipment

- Know the equipment. About 61% of audience members had seen presenters unable to work their own technology at least some of the time, and more than half found it at least somewhat annoying. Have everything ready and coordinate with anyone operating the equipment. **(obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` Test all equipment beforehand. **(expert consensus, 28%)** `[Blome2017]` Design for the disaster: carry the deck as a PDF as well, keep a still-image backup slide for every video, and avoid animations that depend on the host software. **(expert)** `[Naegle2021]`
- Case the room. Visit the place you will speak in before you speak, so that nothing about it is a surprise. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Keep the lights up. Dimming a room signals sleep; ask the audio-visual people to keep it fully lit ("it's extremely hard to see slides through closed eyelids"). **(expert)** `[Winston2018]` The systematic reviewers make the same point against dark slide backgrounds. **(expert)** `[Blome2017]`
- Time of day matters if you can choose it: late morning, when people are awake and not digesting. A room more than half full is better than a big empty one, where everyone wonders where everyone else is. **(expert)** `[Winston2018]`
- Experience with a format does not make you better at it. Presenters' prior experience with slides did not predict how well their slide talk was received. **(exp, within a randomised study)** `[Moulton2017]`
- Logistics: directions to the venue; dressed properly; the visual aids you rely on available; hydrated; know when you return. Last-minute checklist: host organisation, address, contact person and their phone; handout if any; note cards with key words for the opening and closing. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- On arrival: slow down, shake hands, smile, say the opening remarks to yourself at least once. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`
- Afterwards: get feedback from friends; make friends and get feedback. **(own)** `[Freitas2026]`

## 7. Myths and weak evidence

- **"Attention drops after 10 to 15 minutes."** The belief has led institutions to cut lectures to fifteen minutes. A review of the primary studies finds "scant few" of them, the most-cited source "barely discusses student attention at all", the rest have methodological flaws, and the available data do not support a 10 to 15 minute limit. The one consistent finding is that attention varies with the *teacher*, not with the format or the clock. **(obs, review; abstract)** `[Bradbury2016]`
- **"Try to calm down."** Over 90% of people believe it is the best advice for someone about to speak in public; in direct comparison it did nothing for heart rate and produced worse speeches and lower scores than telling yourself you are excited. Calm is the wrong direction: anxiety and excitement are both high arousal, so the short move is sideways, not down. **(exp; obs for the belief)** `[Brooks2014]`
- **"People can hold seven, plus or minus two."** The original figure was a guess with little research behind it; when measured, the number is four, plus or minus one. **(expert, on the memory literature)** `[Conway2014]` `[Kosslyn2012]`
- **"Good slide design is obvious; nobody needs rules."** Tested and false. Shown pairs of slides, one violating a perception principle and one not, people picked the better slide about 80% of the time but could not explain why on a sixth of their correct choices; and every one of 140 real decks violated several principles, academic decks no less than business ones. **(exp; obs)** `[Kosslyn2012]` The most common flaws are the ones most commonly warned against; the systematic reviewers conclude that experts keep giving the advice because presenters keep not following it. **(obs)** `[Blome2017]` Nobody was ever taught to speak: "they told us how to code, they told us how to use git, they didn't tell us how to talk". **(expert)** `[Conway2014]`
- **"Slides make a talk better."** Audiences rated presenters randomly assigned to slides no differently from presenters assigned to no visuals at all, on organisation, engagement, persuasion or effectiveness; only visuals with meaningful motion beat speaking alone. Slides did move a business decision slightly more than no visuals. **(exp)** `[Moulton2017]` Across 28 earlier classroom studies, 17 found no learning difference between slides and traditional lectures, 9 favoured slides and 2 favoured the blackboard; students *preferred* slides in 21 of 26 studies. **(obs, review)** `[Moulton2017]`
- **"Fewer words on the slide means they remember more."** Not by itself: three experiments comparing concise with detailed slides found no retention difference. What worked in the experiments here was replacing text with a stated claim and an explaining picture, moving the words into speech, and keeping only a few signal words. **(exp, second-hand; exp; meta)** `[Blome2017]` `[Garner2013]` `[Mayer2003]` `[Trypke2023]`
- **"No text on slides, ever."** Overstated. Identical text duplicating the speech next to a picture hurts; a few words summarising the speech help, and with no picture on screen at all, text and speech together beat speech alone. The rule the evidence supports is "no transcript", not "no words". **(meta)** `[Trypke2023]` `[Mayer2003]`
- **"The audience wants more text."** They say so, and learn less from it. Students shown bullet-heavy slides rated the amount of text as more appropriate than students shown the sparse assertion-evidence slides (d = 0.60) while performing worse on every comprehension measure; the authors read the rating as a cultural expectation set by PowerPoint's defaults, not a learning need. **(exp)** `[Garner2013]`
- **"A question makes a good headline; it engages them."** In the one direct comparison, the question headline produced less than half the recall of the sentence headline on the same slide. Use a question only when the audience is hostile and needs to see the evidence before the claim, and then reveal the sentence afterwards. **(exp, quasi)** `[Alley2006]`
- **"Add something fun to keep them interested."** Interesting but irrelevant material reliably hurt learning (coherence effect, d = 0.90 across five experiments). **(meta)** `[Mayer2003]` Decorative images reduced recall where representative ones improved it. **(exp, second-hand)** `[Alley2005, citing Carney2002]` `[Blome2017, citing Bartsch2003]`
- **"Animation makes it clearer."** Only when the motion carries the meaning. Movement is the strongest attention magnet available, so decorative motion pulls attention away from the point **(expert)** `[Kosslyn2012]`; motion that shows how the parts relate is the one visual feature audiences rewarded in a randomised test. **(exp)** `[Moulton2017]`
- **"Six elements is the limit."** A widely repeated number from a TEDx talk, demonstrated on stage by counting dots; no study behind it was found, and the effect it demonstrates (subitizing) gives out nearer four. **(expert, unsupported)** `[Phillips2014]` `[Naegle2021]` **(expert, on the memory literature)** `[Kosslyn2012]`
- **"Ninety percent of what you said is gone in thirty seconds."** Stated in the same talk without a source. **(expert, unsupported)** `[Phillips2014]`
- **"There is a TED formula."** Red carpet, childhood story, insider secret, inspiring call to action: used as a formula, these read as stereotype or manipulation. What the good talks share is one idea built in the listener's mind, not a set of moves. **(expert)** `[Anderson2016]`
- **"Building bullets one at a time helps them remember."** Audiences prefer it, but the one direct test found a memory gain on only two of nine items. **(exp, one study, second-hand)** `[Kosslyn2012, citing Mahar2009]`
- **"A famous speaker from a famous place draws the crowd."** Across all TED talks online in 2012, university rank and academic seniority predicted nothing about views, comments or likes. **(obs)** `[Sugimoto2013]`
- **"Giving the big talk will get the work cited."** Academics' citation counts did not rise after their TED talk appeared. **(obs, 18 to 32 presenters per year)** `[Sugimoto2013]`
