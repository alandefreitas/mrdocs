# How to give great talks, in one page

A summary of [HOW-TO-GIVE-GREAT-TALKS.md](HOW-TO-GIVE-GREAT-TALKS.md), one point per section. The full document carries the source tags and evidence marks; this one carries only the conclusions. As of 28 sources read.

- **How to read the document.** Claims are tagged by source and marked by evidence strength: replicated experiments or reviews (meta), a single experiment (exp), observational data (obs), practitioner advice (expert), Alan's own practice (own), abstract-only (abstract). Most experiments are lab studies of students, one research group produced much of the slide-design evidence, and only one study ever randomised presenters to formats.
- **1. What a talk is for.** The job is to build one idea in the listeners' minds; the audience is the hero and the speaker the mentor. Speaking is learnable, and confidence comes from talking about what you know and care about. Decide the objective and the audience first. Slides cannot be notes, visual, handout and paper at once; design for the room and hand out something else. Build from what the audience already knows, with metaphor. Who the speaker is predicts little about a talk's reach; what gets a recording shared is arousal (awe, usefulness, surprise), and a well-received talk makes the speaker look competent.
- **2. Structure.**
  - **2.1 Choosing what to say.** One idea, made the throughline; the audience should be able to name three points a week later. Brain-dump everything you could say, then recognise the five points that form a story, trading importance for narrative fit. Frame the talk as what is versus what could be. Popular science stories pose a question, turn, state their insight, and arouse.
  - **2.2 Opening.** The first thirty seconds decide. Open with a well-rehearsed story that ends at the thing being presented, or with a promise of what the audience will be able to do. Earn attention through curiosity before building. Give a visual roadmap. Sources disagree about opening with a joke. State the questions policy up front. Don't apologise, don't open negative, don't say what they already know.
  - **2.3 Body.** Tell it as a story: flow and hierarchy are why stories are remembered. Alternate what-is and what-could-be. Cycle the central idea from different sides, fence it off from its neighbours, and mark the seams verbally. Signal structure, segment dense material, pre-train the parts before the system. Vary the medium, plant one star moment, model the feeling you want, use materials the audience holds sacred. Every slide should deliver its point to someone who heard nothing.
  - **2.4 Closing.** Restate the idea and give one call to action, then end on the new bliss and go full circle to the opening. Give the ending room. The conclusion slide is the most important slide and stays up through questions; don't waste it on "Questions?" or "Thank you", except in formats without a question period. Sources disagree about ending on "thank you".
- **3. Slides.**
  - **3.1 Words on screen versus in the air.** Slides are looked at, not read; the content lives in the speaker's notes. Text that duplicates the speech next to a picture hurts; a few words summarising the speech help; with no picture, words and speech together beat speech alone. For an expert audience, don't narrate what a self-explanatory picture shows. Never read the slides aloud. Strip titles, logos, backgrounds and slide numbers.
  - **3.2 The one-sentence headline.** The assertion-evidence design (a full-sentence claim over visual evidence, no bullets) improved comprehension and ten-day retention in randomised and classroom tests. A question as headline does worse than a sentence. Writing the sentence forces the presenter to know what the slide is for. Practice is far from this: most slides are phrase headlines over bullets, about 40 words each.
  - **3.3 How much per slide.** One message per slide, and more slides rather than more per slide. Cutting text by itself has weak support; what worked was a stated claim over an explaining picture, speech instead of text, a few signal words, and removing the off-point. About a minute per slide as a ceiling. Working memory holds about four units. Audiences dislike too much and too little text alike, and one deliberately impossible slide is allowed per talk.
  - **3.4 Pictures, diagrams and motion.** Motion that shows how parts relate (panning and zooming across one canvas) was the one visual feature audiences rewarded in a randomised test; plain slides did not beat no slides. Show the picture when its words are spoken. Representative images help, decorative ones hurt. Explanatory animation has no proven advantage over paced discrete steps and can harm; presenter-paced builds with shared elements held in place are the form that works. Show code by highlighting the lines you are saying. Labels on the thing, not in a key. No laser pointers.
  - **3.5 Typography, colour and consistency.** Large type (experts say 24 to 30 point minimum; the higher the floor, the fewer words fit). No all-caps or italic runs, no saturated blue text, no red next to blue, cool background with warm type, colour-blind-safe palettes. Keep the look consistent; change signals meaning. Light versus dark background is the one thing experts contradict each other on. Start from a template that is not the bulleted default.
  - **3.6 Boards, props and the physical world.** A prop is what people remember decades later; a board paces the talk and gives the hands a job.
- **4. Delivery.** Energy transfers to the room. Eye contact is the one delivery habit with an experiment behind it. Face the audience and stand near the screen. Vary the voice, don't rush, articulate. Give the hands something to do. Pause instead of filling. Entertain within your limits. Don't read the slides.
- **5. Questions and answers.** Questions are for the audience; schedule them with a policy announced at the start. Repeat every question. Handle the persistent questioner by deferring them to the corridor. Say "I don't know" when you don't, and after the third, move on. Keep cut detail on slides after the end. Black the screen for discussion. The slide up during questions is the conclusions.
- **6. Preparation, rehearsal and the room.**
  - **6.1 Building the talk.** Writing slides as assertions makes the presenter understand the material better, measurably. Write content before opening slide software. Know the topic; if you were assigned it, go and use it. Review against a perception checklist, because these flaws are not seen by instinct.
  - **6.2 Rehearsal.** Out loud, timed, standing, more than once; never give a talk for the first time to an audience that matters. Rehearse in front of people who do not know the work. Record yourself. Stick to what you practised.
  - **6.3 Nerves.** Relabel the fear as excitement, right before speaking: in four experiments it made speakers more persuasive, confident and competent, while "calm down" did nothing. The arousal stays; excitement is added on top.
  - **6.4 The room and the equipment.** Test the equipment and carry a PDF and still-image backups. Case the room. Keep the lights up. Late morning if you can choose. Prior experience with a format does not predict success with it.
- **7. Myths and weak evidence.** Attention does not drop at ten minutes (it varies with the teacher). The memory limit is four, not seven. Slide design is not obvious. Slides do not make a talk better than no slides. Fewer words alone does not improve retention; "no text ever" is overstated; audiences ask for more text and learn less from it. A question headline underperforms. Fun extras, decorative animation and "six elements" lack support. "Try to calm down" is worse than "get excited". There is no TED formula. Fame and prestige do not predict a talk's reach, and giving the big talk does not raise citations.
