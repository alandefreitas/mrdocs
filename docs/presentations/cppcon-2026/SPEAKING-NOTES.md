# Giving talks

The general craft: how to open, structure, close and deliver a talk. This is the same text as the "Giving talks" note in North (task 778, note 110), kept here so the deck carries its own reference. The talk-specific plan is in [CONTENT-NOTES.md](CONTENT-NOTES.md); the slide-by-slide script is [script.yml](script.yml).

It merges two older notes ("How to start and end a conference talk" and "Giving Talks") with the pattern that won the presentation award at GECCO 2019: minimal slides, lots of motion, and an opening story that the rest of the talk hangs from. Nobody read the paper; the talk did the work.

## The pattern that works

- **Open with a story, prepared better than anything else.** The story ends at the thing you are presenting, so every later slide is "what happened next". Rehearse the first two minutes until they are automatic.
- **Slides are for looking at, not reading.** One image, or one word. If people can read the slide they stop listening to you. The content of the talk lives in your notes and comes out of your mouth.
- **Move things.** Transitions and magic-move animations connect one image to the next, so the picture on screen keeps relating to what you are saying without you having to point at it.
- **Land the one idea, then stop.** One sentence they can repeat. Say it early, cycle back to it, close on it.

## Prepare

- Know the audience. Coworkers or strangers? What do they have in common? Do they have experience with the topic? Do they care, and which facet makes them care: being entertained, instructed, or motivated? What is in it for them? Tell them. Rename the talk to appeal to this audience.
- Choose words. Clear, unambiguous. Paint pictures with stories. Use visuals. Say what is necessary, drop the embellishment. Inclusive: are **we** in this together?
- Length. Less is best.
- Logistics. Directions to the venue. Dressed properly. Visual aids you rely on available. Hydrated. When do you return.
- Last-minute checklist. Host organization, address, contact person and their phone. Handout, if any. Note cards with key words for the opening and the closing. Publicity sheets, if any.

## Structure

1. Printed introduction, if someone introduces you.
2. Opening sentences (see below). Thirty seconds to grab attention.
3. Attention grabber: a large number that makes sense in some light, a physical object, audience interaction ("raise your hand if..."), a contradiction ("do you know diabetes kills more?"), then another contradiction if you can ("do you know I made up these numbers?").
4. Key point. Tell them what you are going to tell them.
5. Let them know what you can do, with data to prove it. Evidence. Benefits.
6. Closing (see below). Tell them what you have told them.

## Opening a talk

1. Stop the applause, if any: "Thank you." (only if there is applause).
2. Catch attention: "Good morning" (flat tone). "Soooooo..." "Alright."
3. Introduce yourself only if the setting is academic: "My name is ___, and I am a ___."
4. Move to the topic. Three safe ways: start a story as if to a child ("Once upon a time", "In 2009", "Once, we had this problem"; always about people), a fact that shocks (Google makes them easy to find), or a question that matters to the audience. Jokes can fail; witty sentences are safer. Show contradictions and exaggerations, connect to the place or the event, keep it fast and snappy.

Openers that work, with examples:

- **Story.** "I grew up to study the brain because I have a brother who..." / "We grew up interacting with the physical objects around us..." / "If we're gonna dive in the deep sea, and..." / "I've been triggered by this question of whether we could evolve or develop a sixth sense." / "About ten years ago, I took on the task to teach global development to Swedish undergraduate students." / "So, I'll start with this: a few years ago, an event planner called me because I was going to do a speaking event. And he said..."
- **Direct statement, neutral or interesting.** "There's nothing bigger or older than the universe. The questions I would like to talk about are..." / "So as researchers, something we often do is use immense resources to achieve certain goals."
- **Provocative.** "I want to discuss with you this afternoon why you're going to fail to have a great career."
- **Curiosity.** "I need to make a confession at the outset here. A little over 20 years ago I did something that I regret."
- **Shocking or weird.** "You will live seven and a half minutes longer than you would have otherwise, just because you watched this talk."
- **Quote.** "I felt a funeral in my brain and..." (Emily Dickinson). We know depression from metaphors.
- **Captivating visual.** "At TED we talk a lot about leadership and how to make a movement. So let's make a movement happen, start to finish, in under three minutes, and draw some lessons from it." / "I'm going to show you a couple of images from a study..."
- **Question.** "Do you think it's possible to control someone's attention? Even more than that, what about predicting human behavior?" / "How do you explain when things don't go as we assume?" / "Brain magic. What's brain magic all about?"
- **Physical object.** "When I was 9, I went to summer camp for the first time and my mum packed me a suitcase." (Have the suitcase.)
- **Relevant joke.** "Okay, now I don't want to alarm anybody in this room, but it's just come to my attention that the person to your right is a liar." / "When you have 22 minutes to speak, 2 million years seem like a really long time."
- **Imagine.** "Imagine a big explosion as you climb through 3,000 feet. Imagine a plane full of smoke. Imagine an engine going clack, clack, clack."
- **Personal experience (less academic).** "I have to tell you I'm both challenged and excited." / "I'm honored to be with you for your commencement from one of the finest universities in the world. Truth be told, I never graduated from college." / "How are you? It's been great, hasn't it? There have been three themes in this conference." / "I'm a writer. Writing books is my profession, but it's more than that." / "I'm really excited to be here today. I'm about to show you some stuff that just came out of the lab."

Don'ts at the start: don't apologize. Don't say something irrelevant or look bad on first impression ("Mmmm... is the mic on?"). Don't start negative ("this next bit is boring, but...", "I'm not good at this, but..."). Don't repeat what they already know ("My name is ___. This talk is about ___."). Ignore latecomers.

Stories add to your popularity, provide anchors, and make you human. Put jokes inside the story only when the story is safe. Look for topical humor from the day of the talk so nobody hears it for the second time. Keep jokes relevant.

## Closing a talk

1. State the key idea clearly, as what you want them to believe or as a call to action. "The lesson I want to leave you with from this data is that our worries are always to some degree overblown and ___." / "We must do this and that." / "The ultimate goal is to ___ and we are making good progress." / "If anyone wants to discuss how we can improve ___, I'll be outside."
2. Call to action and next steps: send me an email, download this, do that.
3. End with a bang and go full circle: come back to the opening metaphor or story. Choose the single most important point of the talk as the final sentence. Find an unexpected or catchy way to state it. Words have power.
4. Signal that you have finished: "That's all I have." / "I rest my case." / "I hope you enjoyed ___."
5. Appreciate: "Thank you for listening." / "Thank you." / "Thank you very much."

## Slides

- Avoid dark colors when the slides will be printed or read on paper; they are hard to read and print. (For a projected or streamed talk, match the venue's palette instead; see [DESIGN-NOTES.md](DESIGN-NOTES.md).)
- Images, and one word at a time. Never more than four bullet points on screen, and ideally none.
- The slide supports what you say; it does not repeat it.

## Rehearse

- Out loud.
- For duration. Not too many slides if the delivery time is flexible.
- Full recital, start to finish, more than once.

## When you arrive

- Slow down.
- Shake hands with people. Smile.
- Say your opening remarks to yourself at least once.

## Delivery

- Leave your arms free to move. Embody the characters in the story.
- Look at one person at a time; change at the pauses.
- Pause instead of saying "mmm". Wait in silence while thinking. Don't say "well...", "so...", "mmm...".
- Articulate clearly.
- Don't judge. Be positive.

## Questions and answers

- Ask for questions that relate to the talk. Leave Q&A to the end, or people interrupt the flow.
- Don't let one person ask many questions or make long comments; interrupt if needed.
- Listen to the whole question. Wait until you are clear about it before answering. If asked without a microphone, repeat the question for the room.
- Don't rush to say "great question": it says the other questions weren't.
- Support answers with facts and figures when you can. Where there is no cast-in-stone answer, preface with "In my opinion...". When you don't know, say so.
- Punctuate answers with a story. Keep eye contact with the questioner.
- If body language says the answer wasn't understood, ask whether they have a follow-up.
- Don't be verbose; don't think aloud while answering.
- Don't end Q&A abruptly: "There's time for two more questions."

## Afterwards

- Get feedback from friends. Make friends and get feedback.
